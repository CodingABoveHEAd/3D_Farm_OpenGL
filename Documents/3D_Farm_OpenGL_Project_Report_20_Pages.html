Project scope and verification

1. Purpose and objectives

The project is an explorable low-polygon rural world designed to demonstrate hierarchical modeling, reusable geometry, interactive navigation, frame-rate-independent animation, lighting, transparency, collision, and scene-scale optimization. Its current result is substantially larger than a single model: a central road connects repeated but varied farms, village amenities, ponds, forests, barns, traffic, people, animals, and an electrical distribution site.

The implementation goal is educational clarity. Objects are built procedurally from cubes, spheres, cylinders, planes, and line-like struts; transformations remain visible in the source; animation state is updated separately from rendering; and controls expose scene systems directly. This makes the program suitable for demonstrating both OpenGL fundamentals and the practical problems that arise in a populated world.

2. Evidence and method

This report was produced by reading the complete current include/, src/, tests/, build configuration and README; configuring and compiling the Release target; running the automated test; launching the graphical application; exercising state toggles; and capturing the client area. Claims are tied to the current code rather than old phase descriptions.

LibrariesGLFW 3.5.1 configuration package and Windows opengl32 through OpenGL::GL.

3. Scope boundaries

The active renderer intentionally does not use GLSL shaders, VBOs, VAOs, image textures, a general shadow system, true reflection, or refraction. Visual richness comes from geometry, materials, lighting, fog, blending and animation. Barns are the only world structures with camera collision. These boundaries are documented as limitations, not silently described as implemented functionality.

Barn interaction, collision and power

Figure 7a. Night, grid power on: exterior and interior warm illumination.
Figure 7b. Same view after load shedding: electric fixtures and hay illumination fade.

1. Interaction controls

GToggle all sliding barn doors. Door amount approaches the target smoothly at about 0.58 units/s.
Z/XHold to move the wheelbarrow toward the entrance/rear. Travel is clamped to approximately −1.35…+1.10 and wheel spin follows distance.
CToggle chest lid; a bounded scalar drives hinge rotation at about 1.25 units/s.
FToggle feeding gate; its hinge parameter approaches the target at about 1.05 units/s.
LToggle load shedding. A smoothed powerAmount fades grid-dependent lights rather than switching them discontinuously.

2. Door-aware camera collision

The scene transforms camera motion into each barn’s local coordinates, samples a long movement in smaller substeps, and rejects samples that intersect wall bands. The front wall is treated specially: its opening half-width grows with door animation, capped near 3.35 units. A closed door blocks the center; a sufficiently open door admits the camera. The resolved local point is transformed back to world space.

Substepping uses steps=ceil(travel/(0.14*barnScale)), avoiding tunneling. For barn world offset (wx,wz), rotation r and scale s, local position is lx=(cos(r)wx-sin(r)wz)/s, lz=(sin(r)wx+cos(r)wz)/s. Wall-band tests run in this reusable local frame.

3. Independent and dependent energy

Barn bulbs are grid-dependent. The power comparison above shows their emissive surfaces, interior visibility and local-light intensity fading together during an outage. Manual door/prop interactions remain available because they are not modeled as electrical actuators. Bonfires likewise remain lit; their flames and point lights are intentionally independent of the grid.

Current limitation. Collision is implemented for barns only. Fences, trees, vehicles, shops, water and substation geometry remain non-solid to the camera.

Ponds, animated water and bridge

Figure 8a. Rural wooden bridge, layered bank and forest setting.
Figure 8b. Water surface, duck, lily pads, reeds, stones and circular ripples.

1. Pond composition

Three ponds break up the repeated farm grid. Each combines an irregular-looking layered soil/sand perimeter with water, stones, reeds and grass. The main and secondary sizes are parameterized (about 36 × 26 and 32 × 23 world units), while placement and surrounding details create distinct landmarks.

The water is an eight-ring, 48-segment translucent polar mesh. At angle a and normalized radius s, x=rx*edge(a)*s*cos(a), z=rz*edge(a)*s*sin(a). Height/color shimmer uses w1=sin(1.6t+1.4x+1.1z) and w2=sin(2.3t-0.9x+1.7z). Ripples expand from radius=0.05+phase*0.14*min(rx,rz) while alpha falls as 0.55(1-phase).

2. Bridge construction

The active bridge in Pond.cpp includes piles, side beams, 13 deck planks, posts and two sampled rails per side. Contrary to the earlier draft, it does not use a cubic Bezier. Its actual arch is:

z(t)=(2t-1)(0.47L),   y(t)=base+rise*sin(pi*t),   0<=t<=1

Twelve neighboring samples become boxes. Midpoint sets translation, sqrt((Delta z)^2+(Delta y)^2) sets length, and -atan2(Delta y,Delta z) sets X rotation. Ducks use elliptical paths (rx*0.42 cos a,rz*0.40 sin a); velocity supplies heading. Lily pads bob by 0.004sin(1.2t+seed).

3. Render-state discipline

Opaque banks, rocks and bridge render with normal depth writes. The water/effects pass enables alpha blending and restores state afterward. Ripples sit slightly above the surface to avoid z-fighting. This ordering is important because legacy transparency does not automatically sort fragments.

Module distinction. The standalone Bridge.cpp belongs to the inactive FarmWorld path. The bridge visible in Figures 8a–b is the independently implemented active bridge in Pond.cpp.

Electrical infrastructure and load shedding

Figure 9a. Fenced substation building, warning signs, conductors and equipment.
Figure 9b. Powered night scene with road lamps and vehicle lighting.

1. Substation / PowerSubstation.cpp

The active fenced yard contains slab/gravel, operations building, roof equipment, door/windows/louvres, warning signs, stack, two transformers, fire walls, gantry, current/voltage transformers, surge arresters, bus and cable equipment, floodlights, generator, drums, kiosk, storage and earth straps. Gate mesh, tubes and beams are generated geometrically. A segment from A to B uses midpoint (A+B)/2, length |B-A| and an axis-angle alignment.

2. Distribution corridor

Utility poles repeat down both road sides. Crossarms and insulators support multiple conductors. Instead of straight lines, each wire span samples a parabolic sag term:

y(t) = (1−t)y0 + ty1 − 4s t(1−t)

The factor 4t(1−t) is zero at each pole and one at the midpoint, giving controllable maximum sag s. This inexpensive curve strongly improves depth and realism.

3. Dynamic equipment and load shedding

Roof fans rotate from animation time, beacons use phase=fmod(t,1.5), and six stack-smoke puffs use life=fract(raw), radius=0.16+0.42life, alpha=0.70(1-life). L changes a target power state; blend=1-exp(-5 DeltaT) and power+=(target-power)blend smoothly fade electrical output.

Road lamps, shop strings/lanterns where electrically modeled, farmhouse/barn fixtures and substation indicators scale with this value. Bonfires remain independent. The scene also applies restrained nighttime outage darkening to terrain so the visual story remains coherent without making the world unreadable.

Inactive alternative. PowerPlant.cpp is compiled but not instantiated by the active Scene. It must not be confused with the visible PowerSubstation.

Sky, weather and environmental effects

Figure 10a. Viewer-facing sun and cloud field.
Figure 10b. Layered flame, stone ring and log seating.
Figure 10c. Moon, night fog and local illumination.

1. Sky and celestial bodies

The sky is drawn with camera rotation but without translation, so it appears infinitely distant. Day color, night color and linear fog interpolate from the same day/night amount. The sun is a camera-facing disk with three restrained glow layers. It uses the same DayNightSettings::SunPosition as the directional light, preventing a common mismatch between visible sun and shading direction. Depth writes are disabled for the translucent glow.

At night, a moon disk/crater treatment and deterministic star field fade in. N changes the target; night=clamp(night +/- DeltaT/3,0,1). The sky view uses camera rotation but omits translation, so the dome/celestial bodies cannot be walked past.

2. Clouds and birds

cloud.cpp distributes 96 clouds uniformly over an annulus: r=sqrt(rmin^2+u(rmax^2-rmin^2)), x=r cos a, z=r sin a. Each moves by p+=direction*speed*DeltaT and wraps to the opposite rim. Day birds use travel=fract(t/period+phase), V formations and wing=32sin((5+0.35f)t+phase); a sparse night pair crosses the moon during 38% of a 22-second cycle.

3. Bonfire system

Five clearings are defined at fixed world positions. Each site contains a stone ring, crossed round logs, ash/embers, four layered drifting flame forms, two smoke puffs, five sparks and nearby villagers seated on split logs. B toggles all sites. At night, nearby active sites can contribute warm, flickering point lights to the ranked local-light set; their illumination is not tied to load shedding.

Smoke/spark life is fmod(rate*t+phase,1). Smoke rises to y=1.55+2.4life, expands and fades; sparks rise to 1+2life with sine/cosine drift. Flame and selected local-light intensity use two sine frequencies. Villagers reuse the seated/standing human model. B toggles all sites.

Fog/transparency. Linear fog blends distant trees toward the interpolated sky color. Clouds, glow, smoke, flames, water and light pools use alpha blending with explicit depth/state restoration.

Rendering, materials and lighting

1. Rendering pipeline

The application requests OpenGL 2.1 and uses the legacy compatibility pipeline: immediate-mode vertex submission, matrix stacks, fixed-function materials, GL_LIGHTING, fog and display lists. There are no GLSL programs, vertex/index buffers or vertex array objects. This makes the transformation and lighting model explicit for study, although it limits modern GPU efficiency.

2. Opaque and transparent passes

StageState and purpose
Frame setupClear color/depth buffers; apply projection and view; configure fog and global light state.
SkyCamera translation removed; celestial glow uses blending and disabled depth writes where required.
Opaque worldDepth test/write on; ground, road, buildings, farms, vegetation, props, characters and infrastructure.
Local illuminationSelect and configure up to seven additional point/spot lights around the camera.
Transparent effectsWater, smoke, cloud layers, particles and projected light pools use source-alpha blending; state is restored.

3. Lighting equations applied to objects

GL_LIGHT0 is directional (w=0). Direction and ambient/diffuse/specular colors use C=(1-n)Cday+nCnight, where n is night amount. Fixed-function shading is conceptually I=KaLa+KdLd max(N dot L,0)+KsLs max(R dot V,0)^shininess. N is the supplied surface normal; GL_NORMALIZE repairs its length after scaled cows, trees, vehicles and buildings.

Point/spot contribution falls by 1/(1+kL*d+kQ*d^2). Candidate farmhouse, shop, barn, road-lamp, bonfire and headlight sources are ranked by priority then squared camera distance; at most seven occupy GL_LIGHT1...7. Headlights also use spot direction/cutoff/exponent.

4. Visual approximations

Bulbs/flamesEmissive material makes a source look bright; selected sources also illuminate geometry.
Light poolsTranslucent ground geometry suggests spill beyond the small hardware-light budget.
WaterAlpha, brightness variation and ripple geometry; no reflection/refraction render targets.
ShadowsNo active general shadow pass. Cows include a simple flattened dark contact shape; other depth cues come from lighting, overlap and fog.
TexturesNo active texture assets; surface identity is built from color/material and additional geometry.
Why the approach works. The scene invests in silhouette, hierarchy, color contrast and secondary motion. Those choices produce recognisable objects even without textures or programmable shading.

Transformation, camera and projection mathematics

1. Model transformations

Vertices begin in local object coordinates. A typical hierarchy uses translation T, rotation R and scale S. With OpenGL’s post-multiplied matrix calls, a vertex is effectively evaluated as:

pworld = T · R · S · plocal

The practical order is therefore scale the primitive, rotate it into orientation, then place it in the parent/world. Nested glPushMatrix/glPopMatrix pairs establish a local coordinate frame for a farm, tractor, cow, villager, barn or shop and safely return to the parent afterward.

2. Camera basis

Let yaw be ψ and pitch be θ, converted to radians. The camera derives:

forward = (cosψ cosθ, sinθ, sinψ cosθ)
right = (−sinψ, 0, cosψ)
up = normalize(right × forward)

The view matrix places the orthonormal basis in the current matrix and translates by the negative camera position. The sky view uses the same basis but omits translation. Keyboard walking intentionally uses only the horizontal heading (cosψ, sinψ); looking upward never causes W/S to fly.

Input axes are summed and the combined direction is normalized only when its length exceeds one. Thus diagonal vertical/forward movement is not faster. Position integration is direct:

positionnew = positionold + direction · speed · Δt

Base speed is 20 units/s; Left Shift multiplies by 3 and Left Ctrl by 0.3. Yaw changes at 90°/s from A/D or arrows, mouse sensitivity is 0.08°/pixel, pitch is clamped to ±89°, and yaw wraps into [−180°, 180°]. Frame time is capped at 0.25 s to contain focus/load spikes.

3. Perspective projection

For vertical field of view f = 45°, aspect a = width/height, near distance n = 0.1 and far distance F = 1000:

top = n · tan(f/2),   right = top · a;   glFrustum(−right, right, −top, top, n, F)

The framebuffer-size callback recomputes this projection after resize/fullscreen changes. A nonzero height guard prevents division by zero.

4. Frustum and distance culling

The scene multiplies projection and model-view matrices, extracts six planes using row 4 ± row 1/2/3, then normalizes them. A bounding sphere is outside when plane·center + d < −radius. Squared distance tests avoid square roots for broad culling. Combined with fog, this prevents large off-screen/distant groups from consuming full draw cost.

Simulation, curve and collision mathematics

1. Frame-rate-independent animation

Most linear state follows x ← x + vΔt and is clamped or wrapped. Periodic motion uses sin(ωt + φ), where a deterministic phase φ desynchronizes instances. Smooth target following uses:

α = 1 − e−kΔt,   x ← x + (target − x)α

This exponential form is used for power and response values because it remains stable across frame rates. Day/night uses a bounded linear progression of about Δt/3. Global pause stops the shared animation progression; traffic and workers additionally expose subsystem pauses.

2. Vehicle and rotational motion

A steered vehicle advances along its heading h with (Δx,Δz)=d(cos h,sin h). Wheel angle is tied to traveled distance, conceptually Δangle = 180d/(πr) degrees. Barn wheelbarrow code uses an equivalent calibrated factor near 150.78° per travel unit. Road vehicles add speed×Δt on their route and wrap only while preserving safe spacing.

3. Procedural distribution

Stable integer/hash seeds derived from chunk, farm or object coordinates choose placement jitter, scale, cow coat layout, timing phase and vegetation. Startup RNG chooses chicken counts, balanced worker populations and bench occupancy once. World grass is generated in 40-unit chunks and rejected by analytical road, path, building, pond and fire-clearance regions.

4. Curves and particles

Active bridge railz(t)=(2t-1)0.47L, y(t)=base+rise sin(pi t); samples form oriented boxes. The cubic Bezier in standalone Bridge.cpp belongs to inactive FarmWorld.
Wire sagLinear endpoint interpolation minus 4s t(1−t).
Particle lifelife = fract(rate×time + phase); smoke rises/expands and alpha falls with life; sparks combine upward life with sine drift.
Viewer-facing disksSun/moon geometry is rotated toward the current view so a thin disk remains legible.

5. Barn collision transform

For a barn translated/rotated/scaled in world space, camera offset is inverse-rotated and divided by scale:

lx = (cosr·wx − sinr·wz)/s,   lz = (sinr·wx + cosr·wz)/s

Local wall bands and the animated opening can then be tested with simple comparisons. The accepted point is rotated/scaled back. This is cheaper and clearer than testing world-space triangles and naturally reuses one collider for every transformed barn.

Numerical care. Capping Δt, clamping bounded states, wrapping angles/routes, normalizing vectors/planes, and subdividing long collision motion prevent common instability and tunneling problems.

1. Implemented optimization

TechniqueWhere it helps
Display listsCache ground/road, farms, vegetation, shop shells, drivers, cloud forms, barns and substation static geometry.

2. Measured runtime and validation

A final 10-second instrumented run in the available review environment, after a five-second warmup with VSync disabled, reported 6.42 FPS / 155.852 ms per frame. The workload is CPU-heavy because it submits large volumes of immediate-mode geometry; the value is environment-specific and is not a universal hardware benchmark.

The Release build succeeded. CameraControlTests passed 1/1 and covers heading-relative movement, opposing-key cancellation, yaw wrapping, immediate stopping, simultaneous curved motion and mouse/keyboard yaw continuity. It does not test drawing, scene toggles, collision or visual output. Manual smoke validation covered startup, windowed/fullscreen/restore/resize, day/night, power, barn interaction and clean exit.

3. Known limitations and maintenance notes

  • Legacy immediate mode and display lists limit performance and portability relative to modern buffered rendering.
  • No active shaders, texture mapping, general shadow maps, reflections, refraction, skeletal animation or physics engine.
  • Camera collision applies only to barns; all other geometry can be traversed.
  • All barn instances share one interaction state rather than per-barn state.
  • Startup randomization means worker/chicken/bench occupancy varies between launches, though it remains stable within one run.
  • FarmWorld, standalone bridge/power plant/texture manager, Shadow, and several helper paths are inactive or dead code.
  • farmworld.cpp includes FarmWorld.h while the repository filename is farmworld.h; this is a case-sensitive-platform hazard.
  • .gitignore matches test*; the present tests/CameraControlTests.cpp is locally available and referenced by CMake, but clean-clone tracking should be checked.
  • README contains stale phase and cloud-offset statements; current code is authoritative.

4. Recommended next steps

Highest-value improvements are modern VBO/VAO + shader rendering, batched/instanced repeated geometry, per-barn state, broader collision, transparent-object sorting, expanded automated scene-state tests, and cleanup of inactive modules/case-sensitive includes. A profiling pass should precede aesthetic expansion.

Conclusion, viva notes and references

1. Conclusion

The current 3D_farm_OpenGL repository demonstrates a complete interactive scene rather than a collection of disconnected models. Its strongest achievement is integration: reusable primitive-based objects operate inside one navigable world; transforms support twelve varied farm instances; animation connects animals, people, vehicles, water and weather; day/night and power states affect a shared lighting model; and targeted collision makes the barn a genuinely enterable environment.

The project also exposes important engineering tradeoffs. Fixed-function OpenGL makes matrices, lights and materials easy to inspect, but the high immediate-mode submission cost is visible in the benchmark. Stylized geometric effects keep dependencies small, but cannot supply the realism of textured shader pipelines. Documenting those facts strengthens the project: it shows a clear understanding of what was implemented, how it works, and what should change next.

2. Short viva / demonstration guide

  1. Begin at the road and explain the Application → Scene → object modules frame path.
  2. Use W/S, A/D and mouse look; derive the forward vector and show movement is frame-time based.
  3. Toggle N and identify the shared sun direction, sky/fog interpolation and local night lights.
  4. Visit a barn; use G, enter through the animated collider, then demonstrate Z/X, C and F.
  5. Toggle L at night and contrast powered fixtures with grid-independent bonfires.
  6. Show traffic/work subsystem controls and explain display lists, frustum/distance culling, LOD and the seven-light ranking.
  7. Close by distinguishing active Pond bridge and PowerSubstation from compiled-but-unused legacy modules.
